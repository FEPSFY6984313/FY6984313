1. Introduction

Supersonic flight refers to travelling at speeds greater than the speed of sound, offering the potential to significantly reduce international travel times. However, following the retirement of Concorde in 2003, commerical supersonic passenger services came to an end. This was largely due to challenges involving high operating costs, excessive fuel consumption, environmental concerns and the disturbance caused by sonic booms. In recent years, advances in aerospace engineering have renewed interest in developing a new generation of supersonic passenger aircraft. Projects such as NASA's X-59 demonstrate efforts to address some of the technical and noise-related challenges. This article investigates whether advances in aircraft design and propulsion can make commercial supersonic passenger flight environmentally and economically viable.

2. Engineering Challenges

One of the main engineering challenges facing supersonic aircraft is aerodynamic drag. When an aircraft travels faster than the speed of sound, shock waves form around its structure, contributing to wave drag. This increases the aerodynamic resistance experienced by the aircraft, meaning its engines must generate greater thrust to maintain supersonic speeds. Consequently, higher drag can lead to increased fuel consumption, operating costs and carbon emissions. Sun and Smith (2017) identify wave drag, skin-friction drag and lift-induced drag as important components affecting supersonic aircraft performance.

Wing design also plays an important role in aerodynamic efficiency. Highly swept wings can improve performance at supersonic speeds. However, they can be less efficient at subsonic speeds, creating a trade-off between high-speed and conventional flight performance (Sun and Smith, 2017).

Another challenge is developing engines capable of operating efficiently across subsonic, transonic and supersonic flight conditions. The propulsion system must generate sufficient thrust while minimising fuel consumption. This is particularly important because inefficient engines can increase operating costs and environmental emissions.

Furthermore, sonic boom remains a significant obstacle to commercial supersonic aviation. Shock waves produced during supersonic flight can create loud disturbances for communities below, contributing to restrictions on supersonic flights over land. NASA's X-59 research aircraft aims to investigate quieter supersonic flight through an aerodynamic design intended to reduce the perceived intensity of sonic booms. Its findings could help inform future aviation noise regulations (NASA, n.d.).

3. Environmental Impact

One of the biggest environmental concerns associated with supersonic aviation is its high fuel consumption. Due to increased aerodynamic drag and the thrust required to maintain supersonic speeds, these aircraft may consume more fuel per passenger-kilometre than conventional passenger aircraft. This can result in higher carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, contributing to global warming. The European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) identifies increased fuel consumption per passenger-kilometre as a major environmental challenge for future supersonic aircraft (EASA, n.d.).

In addition to CO2, aircraft engines produce nitrogen oxides (NOx), which can influence atmospheric ozone concentrations. Higher cruising altitudes also raise concerns about emissions entering the stratosphere, where water vapour and other pollutants can affect the Earth's climate. The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) identifies high-altitude emissions as an important factor when assessing the environmental impact of future supersonic aviation (ICAO, 2025).

Noise pollution is another environmental concern. Supersonic aircraft can produce sonic booms during flight, potentially disturbing communities below. Airport noise from engines during take-off and landing also remains an issue. Although NASA's X-59 programme aims to reduce sonic boom disturbance, further acoustic testing and community-response research are needed to establish its practical effectiveness (NASA, n.d.).

4. Economic Viability

The high operating costs associated with supersonic aircraft represent a significant barrier to commercial success. Increased fuel consumption, specialised engines and advanced aircraft structures can make them more expensive to operate than conventional passenger aircraft. Klingenberg and Belke (2024) investigated the profitability of supersonic travel and estimated that operating costs per seat could be approximately 50% higher than those of comparable conventional aircraft in their model. This suggests that airlines may need to charge higher ticket prices to recover additional costs.

Despite these challenges, supersonic travel could attract passengers who place a high value on reducing journey times, particularly business travellers and premium customers. However, expensive tickets could restrict demand to a relatively small market. This creates uncertainty over whether airlines could consistently fill enough seats to operate profitably.

Companies such as Boom Supersonic are attempting to reintroduce commercial supersonic travel through their proposed Overture aircraft. However, commercial success remains uncertain because aircraft development, certification, operating costs and passenger demand must all be addressed before widespread adoption becomes realistic.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, advances in aircraft design and propulsion have created new opportunities for the development of commercial supersonic passenger aircraft. Improvements in aerodynamics, engine technology and sonic boom reduction could help overcome some of the engineering challenges that previously limited supersonic aviation. However, significant environmental concerns remain, particularly regarding fuel consumption, carbon emissions and high-altitude atmospheric effects. Furthermore, high operating costs and uncertainty surrounding passenger demand present considerable economic barriers. Although projects such as NASA's X-59 demonstrate progress towards quieter supersonic flight, the widespread return of commercial supersonic passenger services remains uncertain. Therefore, further technological development and environmental improvements are necessary before supersonic aviation can become commercially and environmentally viable.
